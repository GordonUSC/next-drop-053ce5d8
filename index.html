The federal residential clean energy credit (Section 25D, the 30 percent homeowner credit for solar and batteries) is not allowed for any expenditures made after December 31, 2025, under Public Law 119-21. The IRS FAQ lists this termination date; the related 25C home improvement credit ended for property placed in service after the same date.Source: irs.gov, 2025-08-21
Reuters reported in February 2026 that US residential solar companies were preparing for a steep drop in business after the homeowner credit expired, with layoffs, restructurings and some company failures; Enphase said it would cut 160 jobs, about 6 percent of its workforce.Source: reuters.com, 2026-02-05
Washington's solar trade group says that after 25D ended, a large federal credit for home solar is available only through third-party-owned systems (leases and power purchase agreements), which pushes the market away from the cash and loan sales that built Washington residential solar. This is WASEIA's characterization; I did not verify the commercial credit deadlines against the statute, so treat the timing as unconfirmed.Source: waseia.org, undated page, read 2026-11-17
Washington net metering: utilities must offer retail-rate net metering until June 30, 2029 or until net-metered capacity reaches 4 percent of the utility's 1996 peak demand, whichever comes first (RCW 80.60.020). After that a utility may keep it or propose a different tariff. Commerce says several utilities have reached the threshold but only a few have moved away from retail rate.Source: commerce.wa.gov, undated page, read 2026-11-17
The Washington State Academy of Sciences delivered its legislatively directed study of the value of distributed solar and storage in October 2026. It notes Washington utilities reaching the 4 percent cap, a national residential battery attachment rate that rose from 9 percent in 2023 to 25 percent in 2024, and regional load forecasts now above 30 percent growth over the coming decade. Any successor to net metering would come later through the UTC, utility boards or the Legislature.Source: washacad.org, 2026-10-23

Training hours and OSHA 10 cost extra and vary by provider.. Needs OSHA 10, 58 hours of PV training (at least 40 advanced, up to 18 from non-accredited sources) and documented project credits. With 330+ projects the experience is covered; the training hours and exam are realistic inside 60 to 90 days.The recognized sales credential in solar. Requirements are on https://www.nabcep.org/new-pv-technical-sales-recertication-and-exam-requirements/ ; confirm the current handbook before paying.
  • PV Associate (PVA)NABCEP. $25 application plus $125 exam ($150); training providers may charge $125 to $180 for a first test. Days to a few weeks; entry credentialFast, cheap signal for software and program employers, and an active PVA can count toward the 18 non-accredited hours for PVTS per the Midwest Renewable Energy Association's summary.
  • Energy Storage Installation Professional (ESIP)NABCEP. $125 application plus $375 exam. Needs OSHA 30, 58 hours of advanced storage training, proof of a decision-making role, and 6 project credits from the last 2 calendar years (1 credit per system of 1 to 80 kWh). Likely more than 90 days unless the training is already done.Storage is where the growth is. Caution: written for installers; NABCEP decides whether a sales and design role counts as decision-making.
